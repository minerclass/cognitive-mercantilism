## Part 1. The idea

### 1.1 Mercantilism in plain language

Picture a king's treasury: a locked room full of gold and silver coins. From about the 1500s to the 1700s, many European governments measured a country's wealth by how much gold and silver it held. They treated the world's wealth as a fixed pile, so one country could get richer only by taking a larger share from another. Historians call this way of thinking mercantilism.

The policies followed from the belief: sell abroad more than you buy, tax foreign goods, control colonies and trade routes, and keep the bullion at home. Wealth was something to guard, not something to grow. Over time a different view won out, in which wealth grows when more people can make, trade and use things.

This summary is general historical background written for the demo. It is not drawn from a single source.

### 1.2 The comparison: cognitive mercantilism

In an essay published October 25, 2026, Venkatesh Rao argued that "AI panic is really a form of cognitive mercantilism" (Rao, 2026). Drawing on Joyce Appleby's history of capitalism (Appleby, 2010), Rao lists four early modern beliefs about wealth and shows each one returning as a belief about intelligence:

| Belief | About wealth, then | About intelligence, now |
|---|---|---|
| There is only so much | "Wealth was fundamentally scarce and finite." (Rao, 2026, summarizing Appleby, 2010) | "Intelligence is imagined as a fixed resource embodied in human brains and the knowledge products mined from them." (Rao, 2026) |
| Only the approved can be trusted with it | The poor had to be governed by force; prosperity was safe only under elite control. (Paraphrase of Rao, 2026) | "The assumption is that intelligence is safe only in the hands of those already certified to possess it." (Rao, 2026) |
| What people want doesn't count | Economic thinking centered on supply, not on ordinary people's wants. (Paraphrase of Rao, 2026) | Job-loss thinking assumes "we demand as much right now as we could ever demand" (Whittemore, 2026a, 00:07:00) |
| Most people aren't good for much | Most people were fit only for farm labor. (Paraphrase of Rao, 2026) | "The cognitive mercantilist assumes that most people are not good for much intellectually." (Rao, 2026) |

Rao's summary image: "The cognitive mercantilist wants to hoard intelligence and its products like bullion" (Rao, 2026).

### 1.3 How Nathaniel Whittemore frames it

On November 1, 2026, Nathaniel Whittemore (NLW) read Rao's essay on *The AI Daily Brief* and added his own commentary (Whittemore, 2026a). His framing is the most useful part for a school audience, because he names the fear directly and then takes it apart.

- **The fear is about relevance, not only jobs.** He describes the anxiety as being about "what it means for our own relevance" (Whittemore, 2026a, 00:00:00).
- **The error is fixed demand.** He calls the assumption behind much AI job-loss thinking "the lump of labor fallacy that runs through so much of thinking about AI job loss" (00:07:00), the belief that "we demand as much right now as we could ever demand" (00:07:00), which he calls "completely ahistorical" (00:08:00).
- **Access creates demand.** One of the kinds of demand elasticity he describes is access elasticity: "Access elasticity, I wanted it, but I couldn't get it" (00:08:00). In an earlier episode he lays out six kinds: price, access, complexity, continuity, personalization and relational (Whittemore, 2026b; checked from a summary only, see 1.6).
- **The backlog is effectively endless.** He argues that "there is always a basically never-ending set of work to be done" (00:16:00), and that when expertise gets cheap, "The entirety of the infinite backlog becomes unlockable" (00:17:00).
- **Transitions still cost something.** "Transitions between orders, even if the order that results is a good one, are never without cost." (00:26:00)

Timestamps come from the episode's published transcript and are accurate to the minute.

### 1.4 Scarcity versus access

The demo's spine is a contrast between two ways of seeing a valuable resource. The rows below are the demo's own summary, not quotations.

| Question | Scarcity view: keep it scarce, hold it close | Access view: open it up, protect people |
|---|---|---|
| How much is there? | A fixed supply. If a machine does it, people are worth less. | It can grow. When expertise is easier to reach, people find more uses for it. |
| Who should hold it? | People already certified: experts, the honors track, a few departments. | Anyone who can use it well, with support to learn how. |
| What do limits look like? | Gates: access by credential, track, budget or role. | Guardrails: limits that name who is protected and from what. |
| What do they fear? | Most people will misuse it, or it will make human minds useless. | Named risks: student privacy, security, and learning that gets skipped. |

### 1.5 Guardrail or gate: the test

The access view does not mean no limits. Student privacy law, security controls for AI agents and age rules protect people, and they belong on the access side. The useful distinction is between two kinds of limit:

- **Guardrail:** names who or what it protects and from what harm, and is no broader than it needs to be. It keeps access open while reducing a specific risk.
- **Gate:** decides who gets access by status, such as a credential, a track, a budget or a role, without naming a harm.

Three questions sort one from the other:

1. Who or what does this limit protect?
2. From what specific harm?
3. Would a smaller limit do the same job?

A limit that answers all three clearly is a guardrail. A limit that cannot answer the first two is a gate. Many real decisions are some of each. This test and its wording were written for the demo; they are not taken from Rao or Whittemore.

### 1.6 Where the comparison strains

These caveats should travel with the demo and be said out loud when it is presented.

- **The historical claim behind Rao's optimism is disputed.** Rao relays Appleby's account that working days rose from about 250 to 300 a year before industrialization, partly so households could buy new goods, and calls today's version a cognitive industrious revolution (Rao, 2026). Clark and Van Der Werf (1998) find no rise in English working days from the Middle Ages to the nineteenth century. Humphries and Weisdorf (2019) support a long-run rise in labor input, but its cause is in question. The underlying thesis is de Vries (2008).
- **"Voluntary" describes some households, not the system.** The goods in that story (sugar, tobacco, tea, cotton) came through slavery and colonial extraction.
- **Scarcity can be produced, not only imagined.** AI is not finite like gold, but access to it is still rationed by licenses, computing power and contracts held by a few companies.
- **More demand is not the same as better thinking.** Growth in use does not show who profits from it, or whether the people using the tools become better thinkers. Handing thinking to a machine does not, by itself, build capability.
- **Transition costs fall on real students.** In a school, the cost of a transition lands on whoever is enrolled while it happens.

## Part 2. Build spec

### 2.1 Goal

A static, accessible web page for conference sessions and workshops. It should work for an audience that has never heard of Rao, Whittemore or mercantilism, and it should make the scarcity-versus-access contrast visible before anyone reaches a scenario.

### 2.2 Hard rules

1. **Static only.** Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript. No build step, no framework, no external fonts, scripts or CDNs.
2. **Works offline and from `file://`.** Load data with plain `<script>` tags that set `window.*` globals. Never use `fetch()` for local data, because it fails on `file://`.
3. **Never invent a citation or alter a quotation.** Use the quotes in Appendix B exactly as written. Every quote shows its source, and the timestamp for Whittemore.
4. **Label every excerpt by kind:** "Quotation," "Paraphrase, not a quotation" or "This demo's framing, not a source."
5. **Keep the caveats.** The contested-history panel and the caveats list in 1.6 are required content, not optional.
6. **Accessible.** WCAG 2.1 AA contrast in light and dark themes; every control reachable and usable by keyboard; visible focus; color never the only cue (each view also has a text label and icon); no hover-only content.
7. **No tracking, analytics or data collection.** Votes stay in memory and are not stored or sent anywhere.
8. **Writing style.** Plain language. No em dashes. Avoid stock AI phrasing such as "delve," "leverage," "foster," "transformative," "Moreover," "Furthermore."

Suggested repo name: `cognitive-mercantilism` (public, GitHub Pages from the `main` branch root).

### 2.4 Views

Tabs with hash routing: `#start`, `#then-and-now`, `#sorter`, `#sources`, `#glossary`. Default is `#start`. A persistent header shows a two-item legend: "Scarcity view: keep it scarce, hold it close" and "Access view: open it up, protect people."

1. **Start here.** Vault image (simple inline SVG) and the primer from 1.1. Rao's comparison with the bullion quote. Whittemore in one sentence. Then the scarcity/access table from Appendix A with a two-button switch, "Then: gold and silver" and "Now: intelligence and AI." Close with a "Not every limit is hoarding" card defining guardrail and gate. A "Next" button leads to view 2.
2. **Then and now.** Four belief buttons. Each panel has two columns: a Scarcity column with a "Then: about wealth" card and a "Now: about intelligence" card, and an Access column with "The other answer." Below: a "For the room" question and a collapsible "Where the comparison breaks down." After the four beliefs: a "What the access view predicts" card marked "Contested history," then the transition-cost card.
3. **Guardrail or gate.** A scenario list and a scenario card. The presenter reveals the three test questions' answers one at a time, records the room's call (Gate, Some of each, Guardrail), then reveals a line from scarcity to access showing the room's call and the starting lean, plus "Read it as a gate (scarcity view)" and "Read it as a guardrail (access, with protection)." Include Previous, Next scenario and Start this one over.
4. **Sources and caveats.** The caveats list from 1.6 and the APA references, each with its check status.
5. **Glossary.** All terms from Appendix E, sorted alphabetically.

### 2.5 Design

- **Two colors carry the argument everywhere:** amber for scarcity (gold in a vault) with a closed-lock icon, teal for access with an open-lock icon. A third, muted purple is used only for "Some of each."
- Define colors as CSS custom properties on `:root`, with a dark set under `@media (prefers-color-scheme: dark)` and a manual Light or dark toggle.
- A "Large text" toggle raises the base font size for projectors. Store toggles in `localStorage` inside `try/catch`, and make the page work without storage.
- Layout works at phone width (16px side gutters, no horizontal scroll). The scarcity/access table stacks into labeled cards below about 700px.

### 2.6 Glossary markup

In any data string, `[[shown words|key]]` (or `[[key]]`) becomes a button that opens the definition from `window.GLOSSARY[key]` in place, directly below the term. It toggles with `aria-expanded` and `aria-controls`, and Escape closes it. Static HTML paragraphs marked `data-rich` get the same treatment on load.

### 2.7 Accessibility details

- Skip link to main content. Tabs use `aria-current="page"`.
- When the presenter reveals an answer, move focus to it. After a vote, move focus to the comparison. When changing scenario or belief, move focus to its heading.
- The scarcity-to-access line has `role="img"` and an `aria-label` stating both positions in words.
- The scarcity/access table uses table roles (`table`, `row`, `columnheader`, `rowheader`, `cell`).
- The Then/Now switch uses `aria-pressed` and announces the change through a polite live region.

## Appendix C. Guardrail or gate scenarios (`data/scenarios.js`)

The "lean" is a starting point for discussion, not an answer key. Scenarios were written for this demo.

### 1. An IEP in a public chatbot (`iep-public-chatbot`, lean: guardrail)

- **Scenario.** A teacher wants to paste a student's [[IEP|iep]] into a free public chatbot to draft accommodations. The district blocks this.
- **Who or what it protects.** A specific student's records, which [[FERPA|ferpa]] covers and which, in Illinois, [[SOPPA|soppa]] limits to operators with a district agreement.
- **From what harm.** Disclosure of special education records to a vendor with no contract, retention limits or deletion terms.
- **A smaller limit?** A district-licensed tool with a signed data agreement does the same drafting job without the exposure.
- **Read as a guardrail.** The restriction is about the tool and the data, not the teacher's ability.
- **Read as a gate.** Little, unless the district offers no approved alternative and the block becomes a ban on the work itself.

### 2. Honors students only (`honors-only`, lean: gate)

- **Scenario.** Only students in honors courses may use the district's licensed AI writing assistant.
- **Who or what it protects.** No named student, record or system.
- **From what harm.** None named. The rationale offered is that other students would misuse it.
- **A smaller limit?** Course-level expectations and teacher guidance apply to every student without sorting them by track.
- **Read as a guardrail.** A pilot with a small group can be a reasonable way to learn before scaling, if it is time-limited and the criteria are open.
- **Read as a gate.** Access follows a credential. This is the [[cognitive mercantilist|cognitive-mercantilism]] assumption that intelligence is safe only with people already approved.

### 3. An agent in the gradebook (`agent-gradebook`, lean: mix)

- **Scenario.** A teacher wants to give an [[AI agent|ai-agent]] write access to the gradebook so it can enter scores automatically. The district says no.
- **Who or what it protects.** Grade records and the [[student information system|sis]].
- **From what harm.** A manipulated or mistaken agent changing grades at scale, with no human review.
- **A smaller limit?** Read-only access, or an agent that drafts entries a teacher approves before they post.
- **Read as a guardrail.** [[Least privilege|least-privilege]]: an agent should reach only what its role requires, and write access to grades is high stakes.
- **Read as a gate.** A flat no, with no path to a reviewed version, keeps a time-saving tool from every teacher.

### 4. One school gets the tutor (`one-school-license`, lean: gate)

- **Scenario.** An AI tutoring tool is available at one elementary school because its parent organization paid for the license. Other buildings do not have it.
- **Who or what it protects.** No named student, record or system. The limit is budget.
- **From what harm.** None named.
- **A smaller limit?** Not a control question. The district can decide whether building-level purchases of instructional tools are allowed at all.
- **Read as a guardrail.** Budgets are real, and a district may not be able to fund every tool everywhere at once.
- **Read as a gate.** Access follows a building's ability to pay. Scarcity here is produced by funding, not by any risk to students.

### 5. District accounts only (`district-accounts`, lean: guardrail)

- **Scenario.** Staff may use AI chatbots only through district [[single sign-on|sso]] accounts, not personal accounts.
- **Who or what it protects.** District data, staff communications, and the logs the district needs if something goes wrong.
- **From what harm.** Work data held under consumer terms, with no retention limits, audit trail or offboarding.
- **A smaller limit?** This is already the narrow version: the tool stays available, and only the account changes.
- **Read as a guardrail.** A standard cybersecurity and records control that keeps access open.
- **Read as a gate.** Only if the district account is so locked down that staff cannot do the work they need.

### 6. Under 13 (`under-13`, lean: guardrail)

- **Scenario.** Students under 13 are blocked from a chatbot whose terms of service require users to be 13 or older.
- **Who or what it protects.** Young students, and the district's compliance with vendor terms and [[COPPA|coppa]].
- **From what harm.** Collecting data from children under terms that do not allow it.
- **A smaller limit?** A tool designed and contracted for younger students, used with a teacher.
- **Read as a guardrail.** The vendor's own terms set the line, and developmental fit matters in elementary grades.
- **Read as a gate.** If no age-appropriate option is offered, younger students simply go without.

### 7. Adults yes, students no (`adults-only`, lean: gate)

- **Scenario.** Teachers use AI tools for lesson planning every day. Students are banned from all AI use, and no learning reason is given.
- **Who or what it protects.** No named student, record or system.
- **From what harm.** None named.
- **A smaller limit?** Task-level rules: AI allowed for some steps and not others, with the reason stated for each.
- **Read as a guardrail.** Some tasks should be done without assistance, and teachers can say which ones.
- **Read as a gate.** A blanket rule for students paired with open use for adults treats the same tool as safe for one group and not the other.

### 8. An unaided writing sample (`unaided-writing`, lean: guardrail)

- **Scenario.** A teacher collects one in-class, AI-free writing sample each quarter to see what students can do on their own.
- **Who or what it protects.** The purpose of the assessment: an honest picture of each student's own writing.
- **From what harm.** A sample that shows the tool's writing, not the student's.
- **A smaller limit?** It is already narrow: one task, with a stated purpose, while AI use continues elsewhere.
- **Read as a guardrail.** This is [[pedagogical friction|pedagogical-friction]] by design. The point of the task is the student's own thinking.
- **Read as a gate.** Only if it grows into a general ban with no stated purpose.

### 9. Who may build agents (`it-built-agents`, lean: mix)

- **Scenario.** Only the technology department may build AI agents that connect to district systems such as email, the [[SIS|sis]] or the help desk.
- **Who or what it protects.** District systems and the data in them.
- **From what harm.** Agents with broad permissions, built without review, that a [[prompt injection|prompt-injection]] or a mistake could turn against the systems they touch.
- **A smaller limit?** A review process for any agent that touches district data, and an open sandbox for tools that touch none.
- **Read as a guardrail.** Connected agents need security review, scoped permissions and logging.
- **Read as a gate.** If the rule also blocks teachers from building tools that touch no student or district data, it protects a department's role, not a system.

## Appendix E. Glossary (`data/glossary.js`)

Definitions written for this demo. Key in backticks.

- **Access view** (`access`): The belief that value grows when more people can use something, so limits should target specific harms, not whole groups of people.
- **AI agent** (`ai-agent`): An AI tool that can take actions in other software, such as sending email or changing records, not only produce text.
- **Bullion** (`bullion`): Gold or silver in bulk, such as bars or coins, valued by weight. Mercantilist governments tried to pile it up at home.
- **Cognitive mercantilism** (`cognitive-mercantilism`): Venkatesh Rao's name for treating intelligence the way mercantilists treated gold: as a fixed supply that should be kept in approved hands.
- **COPPA** (`coppa`): The Children's Online Privacy Protection Act, a U.S. federal law on collecting personal information from children under 13.
- **Demand elasticity** (`demand-elasticity`): How much people's use of something changes when it gets cheaper or easier to get. Whittemore uses it to argue that cheaper expertise means more demand for it, not less.
- **FERPA** (`ferpa`): The Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act, a U.S. federal law that protects the privacy of student education records.
- **Gate** (`gate`): A limit that decides who gets access based on status, such as a credential, a track, a budget or a role, without naming a specific harm.
- **Guardrail** (`guardrail`): A limit that names who or what it protects and from what harm, and is no broader than it needs to be. It keeps access open while reducing a specific risk.
- **IEP** (`iep`): Individualized Education Program: the legal plan for a student who receives special education services.
- **Industrious revolution** (`industrious-revolution`): A historians' term (Jan de Vries) for a claim that households in the 1600s and 1700s chose to work more days so they could buy new goods. The size and cause of that change are disputed.
- **Least privilege** (`least-privilege`): A security rule: give a person or tool only the access its job requires, and nothing more.
- **Lump of labor fallacy** (`lump-of-labor`): The mistaken idea that there is a fixed amount of work to go around, so a machine that does some of it must leave people with less.
- **Mercantilism** (`mercantilism`): A way of thinking about national wealth common in Europe from about the 1500s to the 1700s. It treated gold and silver as the measure of wealth and the world's wealth as a fixed amount, so one country gained only when another lost.
- **Pedagogical friction** (`pedagogical-friction`): Productive difficulty built into learning on purpose, so students do the thinking a tool could otherwise do for them.
- **Prompt injection** (`prompt-injection`): Hidden instructions placed in a web page, email or file that trick an AI tool into doing something its user did not ask for.
- **Scarcity view** (`scarcity`): The belief that something valuable exists in a fixed amount, so sharing it means losing it. Limits follow who someone is.
- **Single sign-on** (`sso`): One district login that opens many approved tools, so the district controls the account and its data.
- **SIS** (`sis`): Student information system: the district database for enrollment, schedules, attendance and grades.
- **SOPPA** (`soppa`): Illinois's Student Online Personal Protection Act, which limits what online services may do with student data and requires written agreements with school districts.
- **Transition cost** (`transition-cost`): The disruption people bear while one way of doing things gives way to another, even when the new way turns out better.
